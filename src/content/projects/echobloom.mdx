<CaseSection id="overview" kind="overview">
  EchoBloom began with a familiar commute problem: traffic, crowds, and
  mechanical noise accumulate into irritation before the day has properly
  started. Instead of designing another noise-blocking tool, I explored whether
  those unwanted sounds could become raw material for something personal.

<RoleBreakdown>
  I defined the product loop, trained and tested the sound-classification model,
  connected the creative tools, coded the reactive visuals, and designed the
  mobile interface.
</RoleBreakdown>

The concept records a short sound, recognizes its likely category, transforms
the result into music and motion, and saves that output as a small memory of a
specific place and time.

</CaseSection>

<CaseSection id="insight" kind="insight">
  The opportunity was not to make the commute silent. Silence would erase the
  context that made each moment distinct. The more interesting direction was
  to give the user authorship over the same stimulus that had been wearing them
  down.

  <InsightQuote>
    A frustrating sound can become meaningful when the user can identify it,
    transform it, and keep the result.
  </InsightQuote>
</CaseSection>

<CaseSection id="decisions" kind="decisions" layout="wide">
  I organized the experience around one short, explainable chain rather than a
  feature-heavy music app. Teachable Machine and the UrbanSound8K categories
  provided a rough sound label; Suno supported musical exploration; p5.js
  translated the result into color and motion.

<ProcessGallery caption="Each tool has one visible job in the user-facing loop.">
  <article>
    <strong>Record</strong>
    <p>Capture a brief fragment from the user’s current environment.</p>

  <article>

<CaseSection id="prototype" kind="prototype" layout="wide">
  The mobile flow was reduced to three moments: record and generate, review the
  reconstructed music with reactive motion, then save or share the result. The
  visual layer intentionally feels atmospheric rather than analytical; model
  status and controls remain secondary to the emotional payoff.

<CaseSection id="reflection" kind="reflection">
  The prototype proves the creative loop, but it does not yet establish robust
  real-world sound recognition or seamless generation latency. A production
  version would need clearer consent around environmental recording, graceful
  handling of uncertain classifications, and a faster preview before full
  music generation.

  <ReflectionBlock title="The technology is most useful when it changes the user’s relationship to the moment.">
    EchoBloom strengthened my ability to move between code, model behavior, and
    interaction design. The next test should compare whether transformation
    actually improves commute mood over repeated use—not only whether the
    output feels novel once.
  </ReflectionBlock>
</CaseSection>
